## The Value of Skilled Work

Essential building materials like steel and cement often get the spotlight in conversations about construction. While these materials are vital, they cannot assemble themselves. Labour, therefore, becomes another important part of the picture.

Nigeria's population offers a strong starting point for this, with over 60% currently under the age of 30. For an industry that depends heavily on people, that should be a significant advantage.

Despite this, skilled workers like bricklayers, carpenters, electricians and plumbers remain genuinely difficult to find, even with unskilled labour widely available. These are the workers whose level of expertise and precision can have a direct impact on the quality and durability of a building. Nigeria also has a growing number of quality control and assurance professionals, but there is a real gap between general graduates entering the field and the certified, deeply experienced specialists the work actually requires.

## The Widening Gap

Nigeria's current Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) push has built out more than 1,600 accredited training centres nationwide and drawn a wave of applications. However, that effort accounts for under 1% of the country's youthful population, with a much smaller share, roughly 0.1%, currently in active training. The country's own Industrial Training Fund has admitted plainly that, despite abundant human resources and educational institutions, Nigeria still imports artisans and skilled workers from Ghana, Togo, Benin Republic, Bangladesh, Pakistan and India to execute technical jobs at home.

That gap also runs through the workforce as a cycle. Unskilled labourers have limited visibility into how they might grow into a skilled trade, and even those already skilled have little sense that there's a level beyond where they currently sit. The pool stays static, with only a few people moving into skilled roles to grow it over time.

Even the people who make it through the system don't always stay in it. Countries like the United Kingdom and Canada face shortages in trades such as bricklaying, carpentry, electrical work, and welding, and what they offer in pay and working conditions is often hard to match at home. Skilled Nigerians are taking notice, and taking the opportunity. Nigeria is losing skilled labour almost as fast as it produces it.

## The Fulcrum View

Nigerian developers with proven track records have spent years building trust and credibility in the industry. That standing gives them an avenue to create pathways for workers to develop their skills. For example, Family Homes Funds, a federal social housing initiative in Nigeria, ran a training programme on one of its housing projects in Abuja and certified over 500 young Nigerians. HBM Nigeria has also trained workers in tiling and block laying alongside its construction work. These programmes show what is possible when developers use the projects they already have to develop the workforce they need.

Another area of growth is scale and clear guidance for the workers going through the system. A labourer who can see a real path into a skilled trade has a reason to stay and improve. A skilled worker who can see a path into levelling up and training others has a reason to keep growing instead of remaining on the same level. Right now, that path closes too early for many people, and the industry risks losing them just as their skills are becoming more valuable.

The real estate industry in Nigeria already has strong platforms where conversations about capital, markets, and growth take place. Workforce development can become part of that conversation too, bringing the people behind the projects into discussions about where the industry is headed and how the skills needed to sustain it can be developed.
