## The Raw Material Advantage

Cement is one of the most essential materials in construction. Almost every construction site has that mixing point where cement, granite, and sand come together to make building blocks. Block industries line up in small rural areas and along major roads across the country. Limestone, the primary raw material for cement manufacturing, isn't scarce either. Nigeria has deposits across Ogun, Kogi, Benue, Cross River, Ebonyi, and several other states, including one deposit near Calabar considered the country's purest.

Nevertheless, wide use isn't the same as wide production. Turning that raw material into a finished product like cement, at scale, is a business only a handful of companies have managed to build in Nigeria. Those companies, though few, have now built more than the country needs. Nigeria's installed cement production capacity currently sits at 60 to 65 million tonnes a year, according to the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC). In comparison, domestic demand is only around 25 to 30 million tonnes. This capacity enables Nigeria to export to countries like Ghana, Cameroon, and Togo, made possible only by a decision the government made in 2002.

## The Question Obasanjo Asked

In 2002, the Backward Integration Policy (BIP) was one of the Obasanjo government's most ambitious industrial strategies, built on a simple idea. Companies that wanted to keep importing raw materials into Nigeria had to also invest in producing them locally, or risk losing the licences that let them import in the first place. It touched flour, sugar, rice, and even sack bags.

The policy reached its peak impact in the cement market because Aliko Dangote, then Nigeria's largest cement importer, saw it as an opportunity rather than an obstacle. The story most often told is that Aliko Dangote was asked why the country wasn't producing its own cement locally. He answered that importing was more profitable.

That exchange set the tone for what followed. It took an industry from relying almost entirely on imports to producing far more than it needed, enough to export the surplus. Local cement production rose from under 2 million tonnes in 2002 to over 45 million tonnes by 2018. That's an increase of roughly 1,400%. By 2015, Nigeria had reached full self-sufficiency in cement, and capacity has kept climbing since.

The policy worked, but the way it worked also shaped what the industry would eventually become.

## Three Players, One Market

Today, three companies control more than 90% of Nigeria's installed cement production capacity. Dangote Cement holds the largest share by far, with BUA Cement and HBM Nigeria, formerly Lafarge Africa, making up most of the rest.

These companies invested capital in the Backward Integration Policy when it was still a risk, and built accordingly. That's part of why Nigeria has the capacity to spare today.

However, a market shaped this way naturally draws attention when prices move. Cement prices have skyrocketed in less than a year. Industry reporting puts the increase at roughly 50% in some parts of the country and closer to double in others. The Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (FCCPC) has opened an investigation to understand what's behind it, whether it's genuine cost pressures like energy prices and naira depreciation, or something tied to how concentrated the market is.

What makes the moment notable is that Nigeria's installed capacity sits at roughly double what the country actually consumes, a surplus that would ordinarily work in the other direction.

The conversation has moved beyond the regulator too. The Real Estate Developers Association of Nigeria has called for government support in stabilising the building materials value chain, with some stakeholders suggesting more cement importers could widen competition. The Housing Development Advocates of Nigeria have made a similar point. When only a few companies supply nearly all of a market, there's little competitive pressure left to keep prices in check.

## The Cost of This Concentration

Three companies controlling nearly all of a country's cement supply don't have their effects contained to cement. It spills over into other industries too, and real estate absorbs much of that.

Renovations and repairs of existing properties get more expensive as prices climb, and the next effect hits anything not yet built. Developers already battling inflation and currency volatility find cement eating into their margins, and the usual response is to slow down or delay projects. That keeps Nigeria's housing deficit, currently around 15 million units, exactly where it has been.

Nigeria has clearly solved the production problem, with enough cement to meet its own demand twice over and surplus left to export. Still, that success never meant the cost of turning that cement into a finished house would fall along with it. Supply and affordability proved to be two different problems, and solving one didn't automatically solve the other.

Some industry voices argue the fix isn't just more cement producers, but reducing how much any single material can dictate a house's cost, through alternatives like bamboo, clay, mud, and others. Either way, a market this important shouldn't rest this heavily on so few hands, especially when the people paying for that concentration are rarely the ones sitting in the boardroom.

## The Fulcrum View

The story on cement isn't one of failure. It's the rare Nigerian industrial policy that did exactly what it set out to do. A country that imported nearly all its cement two decades ago now produces more than it needs, and the companies that made that happen took on real risk when it would have been simpler to keep importing.

That achievement is real, but it's also why the industry looks the way it does now: three companies control how prices move and who gets to build. That's what market concentration produces at this scale, regardless of who's running it.

Although there's a lesson in here that goes beyond cement. Producing a material locally was always presented as the fix, the thing that would eventually bring costs down for the person trying to put up a house. What this industry actually shows is that local production and affordable housing are not the same goal, and solving one doesn't guarantee the other. A country can hit self-sufficiency and still watch building prices rise if the market built to deliver that self-sufficiency never had to compete on price.

The honest question is where a fourth or fifth producer would even come from. Building a cement plant from scratch isn't something most Nigerian companies can finance alone. A more realistic path already exists elsewhere. Development finance institutions have already shown interest in Nigerian cement. The African Development Bank and the International Finance Corporation co-financed a cement plant in Sokoto years ago. However, that funding went to an existing major producer rather than a new one. The tool for funding a new entrant already exists, but nobody has used it yet.

Foreign capital pairing with local players is a promising direction. It is similar to how Huaxin entered through Lafarge, though that route needs watching too, since it can just as easily extend the big three's reach instead of creating real competition. Geography also creates room. Nigeria's limestone deposits span many states, which means the next real entrants may not be national giants at all, but smaller, regionally focused producers serving markets closer to home.

None of it is fast, and none of it guarantees prices will actually come down. However, concentrated industries loosen when a challenger can be built, not when everyone waits for one to show up. When that happens, affordability needs to be part of the plan from day one, not something the market only gets around to later.
