No building in Nigeria stands on concrete alone. Underneath every high-rise, flyover, and major infrastructure project is steel, the part nobody sees but everything depends on. Concrete gives a structure its visible size and shape, but steel is what actually makes that vertical scale possible.

For a Nigerian developer, choosing steel is not just a decision of materials. It means absorbing the risk of a global market Nigeria doesn't control, because steel is an input the country needs for every multi-storey building and every major road or bridge, but one it barely produces itself. That risk comes from things like global price swings, currency devaluation, and the price of shipping steel in from elsewhere. Relying on imported steel works like an invisible tax on the country's development, pushing up the cost of housing and infrastructure by tying local building to global supply chains Nigeria has no control over.

Nigeria has the resources to produce its own steel but still imports the material on which its own growth depends. That contradiction isn't unique to Nigeria. It plays out across the whole continent.

## Africa's Paradox

Africa holds a meaningful share of the world's iron ore reserves, and a far larger share, roughly 30%, of the continent's broader critical mineral reserves overall. Yet by the African Development Bank's own data, Africa produces only about 4% of the world's iron ore, and even less finished steel.

Demand tells a different story. The World Steel Association's outlook puts Africa's steel demand growth at roughly 5.5% a year over the past three years, driven by construction and fast-growing cities. The continent needs more steel every year. It just isn't the one making it.

That gap between big reserves and low output, even as demand keeps rising, isn't about a lack of resources. As the World Economic Forum's 2026 analysis puts it, the real barrier is the absence of a full value chain connecting the ore in the ground to a finished product. Until infrastructure, policy, and funding line up, the continent stays an exporter of raw potential and an importer of the finished product it could be making itself. Nowhere is that truer than in Nigeria.

## Nigeria's Contradiction, and the Plant That Never Ran

In 2023, Nigeria's crude steel output stood at just 1.19 million tonnes, against demand estimated at more than 10 million tonnes a year. The gap is filled by imports valued at more than $4 billion a year, mostly from China, Turkey, India, and Ukraine, where large-scale production keeps prices low enough to beat anything Nigeria could produce at home, even if it tried.

The name behind this contradiction is Ajaokuta. What makes it different from most failed infrastructure stories is that it actually got close. By the early 1990s, the plant was reportedly 98 percent complete, before the Soviet collapse stripped it of the technical support it depended on, and a failed concession to an Indian firm years later stripped it further, ending in a $496 million arbitration payout. Since then, the pattern has mostly repeated itself: new agreements, new site visits, new promises, and no commercial steel to show for any of it. Nigeria is now pursuing a fresh Chinese-backed revival, even as a separate UK infrastructure deal quietly imports finished British steel. This detail has not gone unnoticed by Nigerians who are asking why a country trying to revive Ajaokuta is buying steel from Britain at the same time.

So the real reason behind Ajaokuta is not a lack of ambition, and it is also not that Nigeria failed to invest. It is a plant that was built almost to completion, then lost its technical foundation when the Soviet Union fell apart, and was handed to a partner that stripped rather than strengthened it. It has now spent every year since then caught between new foreign partnerships and the unresolved cost of the last one.

## What the Import Bill Actually Costs

The real story here isn't just Ajaokuta's failure. It's what that failure costs everywhere else. When a country imports most of its steel, its construction industry inherits every risk sitting upstream of that decision, from global steel prices and exchange rate swings to shipping costs, port delays, and import duties. None of that shows up as its own line on a building's cost sheet. It just gets added into the price of reinforcement, framing, and roofing, pushing up the cost of housing, infrastructure, and factories all at once, without ever being called "the steel problem."

That's the real point. Nigeria doesn't just need a steel plant for its own sake. The price and availability of a basic input like steel sets a floor under the cost of building almost everything else the country needs. A country that imports its steel is, in a real sense, importing someone else's cost structure.

Nigeria is not entirely without steel production outside Ajaokuta. Private players have already stepped into the gap the state project left open: a $400 million plant in Ogun State, a Kaduna-based iron ore operation backed by $600 million, and smaller plants in Ilorin and Sagamu, all producing steel right now, without waiting for Ajaokuta to resolve anything. It proves the country doesn't need one plant to fix this. It needs a strategy that doesn't depend on one.

## The Fulcrum View

Solving Nigeria's steel contradiction isn't about nostalgia for what Ajaokuta was meant to be. It's about being honest that the plant's failure was never a story about ambition or missing resources. It was a story about broken partnerships, weak contracts, and decades spent handing the project to whoever offered the next promise, without ever protecting it from the last one's mistakes.

That starts with treating future partnerships differently. Nigeria is currently pursuing a Chinese-backed revival for Ajaokuta at the same time as a separate infrastructure deal brings in finished British steel, a reminder of how many different efforts are already in motion. A single, coordinated strategy would get more out of each of them. Any capital put into Ajaokuta now, including the proposed China-backed revival, needs contracts that are transparent from the start, with clear performance milestones and protections against the kind of asset stripping that ended the last major concession. A plant that got this close to completion once does not need another blank cheque. It needs a partnership built to survive a change in government or a change in geopolitics, which is exactly what broke it before.

It also means Nigeria doesn't need to solve this through Ajaokuta alone. The private steel plants already running in Ogun, Kaduna, Ilorin, and Sagamu prove that domestic production is possible without waiting on a single legacy complex to finally work after more than forty years of not working. A serious steel strategy treats Ajaokuta as one part of a wider push, not the only path forward.

Success shouldn't be measured by whether Ajaokuta specifically is running. It should be measured by whether the cost of steel in Nigeria stops moving with someone else's currency, shipping lanes, and political timeline. A functioning Ajaokuta would be a milestone, but it wouldn't be the finish line.

The steel is already in the ground. What Nigeria has never fully tested is whether it can build a partnership strong enough to outlast the promise that started it.
