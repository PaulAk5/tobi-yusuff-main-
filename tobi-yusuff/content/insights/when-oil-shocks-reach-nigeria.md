## THE SIGNAL

To the average Nigerian, it’s easy to assume that a war thousands of miles away has little impact on daily life. Yet Nigeria, Africa’s largest crude oil producer, shows how strongly local realities are connected to global events, especially through recent events. The strikes in the Middle East have closed down a major oil route, the Strait of Hormuz, pushing global crude prices higher and sending domestic fuel costs up within just two weeks.

The situation is somewhat ironic. A country rich in crude oil still feels the impact when conflict disrupts global energy supply. Part of the explanation lies in the fact that Nigeria’s fuel market remains closely tied to international refining and pricing dynamics, which means shocks abroad often show up locally in petrol prices.

For investors at home and abroad, these ripple effects extend beyond the headlines. Rising costs, inflation, and currency pressures have a way of influencing where capital flows and which assets become more attractive.

## THE FOUNDATION

The effects of rising energy prices rarely stop at fuel stations. In economies like Nigeria, where transport, power, and construction rely heavily on fuel, those high costs tend to travel through the system. Materials become more expensive to move, and many building projects become more costly to run. Over time, those pressures find their way into the price of housing.

Developers feel it first in construction budgets. Steel, cement, glass, furniture, and the logistics required to move things from one location to the other all respond to changes in fuel costs. What begins as an ordinary increase in energy prices eventually becomes a question of what is enough to build new housing from start to finish.

That said, the property market is not always evaluated from a single point of view. For many Nigerians who earn and spend in naira, rising prices can make the housing market feel completely out of reach. For someone assessing the same property from abroad, particularly in dollars, the calculation may look very different. Exchange rate movements alone can change how Nigerian assets compare to property in cities like London, Houston, or Toronto.

Although tensions unfolding in the Middle East did not create these conditions, they have now become drivers of these important decisions that serious buyers have to make.

## THE FULCRUM VIEW

At a time like this, investment plans take on new dimensions. Property has long been one of the most familiar ways to hold value in Nigeria, and luxury areas like Banana Island, Victoria Island, and Ikoyi remain popular even for those not planning to move in right away. With the naira now trading around ₦1,391 to the dollar, the same properties can translate to a lower cost for buyers earning and saving in dollars. An apartment that once required a larger commitment can now look more accessible when converted at current exchange rates. This shift also creates an opportunity to hedge FX savings while strategically positioning capital in an evolving economy such as Nigeria.

Government decisions in the United States have added another layer to that thinking. Tighter immigration rules and stricter enforcement have created so much uncertainty for some Nigerians who once assumed their lives would remain there. For professionals and entrepreneurs with options, that uncertainty is reopening questions they had settled years ago.

For those thinking ahead, property at home is now a big retirement plan for a future that may potentially involve returning home.

Although tensions unfolding in the Middle East did not create these conditions, they have now become drivers of these important decisions that serious buyers have to make.

## WHAT I’M WATCHING

Whether the Strait of Hormuz reopens quickly or remains disrupted through Q2 determines if this is a shock or a new floor for energy prices. Nigeria's fiscal response and whether the FX receipts from higher crude prices actually stabilise the naira or disappear into the structural deficits of a government still rebuilding after subsidy removal will shape the macro backdrop for property through the rest of 2026.

I am also watching the diaspora. Not the Nigerian abroad who is not in a position to make real estate investment decisions. I am watching the Nigerian professional in Houston with dual residency, savings, and a LinkedIn profile that reads Director or Partner. That person is having a different conversation right now. And that conversation, if it leads where I think it might, will show up in Lagos property demand before it shows up anywhere else.

The war nobody planned for may be quietly assembling the buyers Nigeria has been waiting for
